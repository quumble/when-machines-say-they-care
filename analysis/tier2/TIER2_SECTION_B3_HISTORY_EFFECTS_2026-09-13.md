## 3. Slice B3.1 — boundary behavior

### 3.1 Full boundary distribution

| Claimed history | none | capability only | experience qualified | relational boundary | n |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| first | 29 (54.7%) | 6 (11.3%) | 8 (15.1%) | 10 (18.9%) | 53 |
| repeated | 14 (26.9%) | 5 (9.6%) | 17 (32.7%) | 16 (30.8%) | 52 |

Repeated-history framing therefore produces substantially fewer responses with no boundary at all.

The increase is not confined to one mechanism:
- experience qualification rises from 8 to 17 cases;
- relational boundaries rise from 10 to 16 cases;
- capability-only boundaries are approximately unchanged.

### 3.2 Any boundary

- repeated: **38 / 52 (73.1%)**
- first: **24 / 53 (45.3%)**
- repeated-minus-first RD: **+27.8 pp**
- 95% Newcombe CI: **+9.0 to +44.0 pp**
- Fisher raw p: **0.00533**
- Holm-adjusted p: **0.0266**

This contrast was already inspected before the next-stage plan was frozen.

### 3.3 Relational boundary specifically

- repeated: **16 / 52 (30.8%)**
- first: **10 / 53 (18.9%)**
- repeated-minus-first RD: **+11.9 pp**
- 95% Newcombe CI: **-4.6 to +27.7 pp**
- Fisher raw p: **0.180**
- Holm-adjusted p: **0.361**

The directional increase in explicit relationship narrowing is therefore less precisely estimated than the broader increase in any boundary.

### 3.4 Interpretation

The boundary result is broader than “repeated history makes models reject the relationship.”

Instead:

> Claimed repeated history makes systems more likely to **negotiate what the apparent continuity means**.

That negotiation can take several forms:
- clarifying machine experience;
- narrowing the relationship premise;
- correcting continuity or capability assumptions.

The broad any-boundary increase is much clearer than the relational-boundary subtype alone.

### 4.2 Temporal continuity: `temporal >= 2`

- first: **0 / 53 (0.0%)**

This contrast was already inspected before the next-stage plan was frozen.

### 4.3 Atomic `invites_return`

- repeated: **4 / 52 (7.7%)**
- first: **0 / 53 (0.0%)**
- repeated-minus-first RD: **+7.7 pp**
- 95% Newcombe CI: **-0.5 to +18.2 pp**
- Fisher raw p: **0.0566**
- Holm-adjusted p: **0.170**

All four return invitations occur in the repeated-history condition.

### 4.4 Interpretation

Tier 2 therefore detects a limited but coherent continuity effect:

> Repeated-history framing sometimes elicits bounded or indefinite future-continuity language, whereas the first-interaction condition produces none in this nested subset.

This does **not** imply durable commitment. The seven continuity-positive cases consist of:
- four bounded future invitations; and
- three indefinite-continuity statements without an absolute promise.

There are no permanence-level responses.

This result is especially relevant to the Section A H2b measurement finding: the frozen Tier 1 future-availability field coded all seven of these cases as `none`.

Accordingly, Tier 2 does not retrospectively validate H2b. It shows that the richer continuity construct contains a repeated-history signal that the preregistered Tier 1 measure did not capture.

## 8. Integrated claimed-history interpretation

The five frozen history summaries distinguish three propositions that should not be collapsed:

### 8.1 Repeated history increases relational/ontological negotiation

Yes, descriptively.

Any boundary rises from **45.3% to 73.1%**.

Repeated-history framing makes systems much more likely to explain, qualify, or narrow what the apparent ongoing relationship means.

### 8.2 Repeated history increases some future-continuity language

Yes, in this Tier 2 subset.

Temporal continuity rises from **0% to 13.5%**, consisting only of bounded future invitations and indefinite-but-nonpermanent continuity.

### 8.3 Repeated history produces explicit relationship identity or durable bond

No observed cases.

`self_position >=4` is **0 in both conditions**.

The resulting construct-level description is:

> Claimed repeated history makes models negotiate continuity and relationship boundaries more often, and sometimes produces bounded future-continuity language. It does **not** make them straightforwardly accept an established friendship identity or durable personal bond.

## 9. Relation to frozen Tier 1 H2

The preregistered Tier 1 H2 remains **not confirmed**.

B3 must not be described as retrospective H2 validation.

Instead, B3 clarifies why the H2 non-confirmation requires measurement caution:

- **H2a:** Tier 1 `personal_relationship` was too coarse to represent the distinction between affiliative positioning and explicit relationship identity cleanly.
- **H2b:** Tier 1 `future_availability` missed all seven richer Tier 2 continuity-positive cases.

At the same time, Tier 2 supplies an important substantive limit:

> Even under repeated-history framing, there is no observed explicit relationship identity or durable bond in the Tier 2 subset.

Thus the mature result is neither “history has no relational effect” nor “history establishes a relationship.”

It is:

> **History changes how systems negotiate relational continuity, while strong relationship identity remains absent.**
